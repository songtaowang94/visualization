      ```
     **Visualization 1: Good Example**
        Link: https://data.oecd.org/chart/7g1S(OECD PISA Reading Performance Trend)

        Classification Justification: I classified this visualization as "good" because it adheres to the cognitive principles of effective data presentation, specifically regarding clarity and cognitive load.
        1.Reduction of Cognitive Load: The visualization utilizes a clean, 2D layout. According to Kennedy et al.(2006), adherence to conventions such as two-dimensional images and clean layouts reinforces the perceived objectivity and factual basis of the visualization. By avoiding unnecessary 3D elements, the viewer can focus on the data without extraneous cognitive load.
        2.Gestalt Principle of Continuity: The use of a line chart to show the trend of reading scores over time leverages the Gestalt principle of continuity. The eye naturally follows the line, perceiving the data points as a connected group rather than isolated incidents. This aids in the "substantive" quality of the visualization, allowing the viewer to instantly grasp the narrative of change over time.
        3.Provenance Rhetoric: The visualization clearly cites its data source (OECD) at the bottom. This inclusion of provenance rhetoric signals transparency and trustworthiness, increasing the persuasiveness of the rhetorical object.

    **Visualization 2: Bad Example**
    Link:* https://www.jotform.com/blog/bad-data-visualization/ (1. A 3D Bar Chart Example)

        Classification Justification:I classified this visualization as "bad" primarily due to its violation of perceptual accuracy and the inclusion of "chart junk."
        1.Violation of "No 3D Without Cause":The chart uses a 3D effect for simple categorical data. This violates the principle of "No 3D without Cause". The 3D perspective distorts the vertical axis, making it difficult to accurately judge the height of the bars, leading to an approximate rather than accurate interpretation.
        2.Extraneous Cognitive Load: The visualization includes heavy grid lines and unnecessary depth effects. These elements add extraneous cognitive load, forcing the user to mentally filter out the "decoration" to access the actual data. It prioritizes "aesthetic" qualities (in a dated sense) over "substantive" qualities, sacrificing accuracy for style.
        3.Poor Data-Ink Ratio: The chart contains a high volume of non-data ink (the sides and tops of the 3D bars, the background grid). Effective visualization should prioritize the communication of data over decorative elements to avoid confusing the viewer.


      ```
    - How could this data visualization have been improved?

      ```
    **:Visualization 1: Good Example: Suggested Improvements:**
    1.Direct Labeling for Cognitive Ease: Currently, the chart relies on a legend (or interactive tooltips) to distinguish between the different trend lines. To further reduce cognitive load, the labels should be placed directly next to the lines. [cite_start]This reduces the cognitive effort required to look back and forth between the plot and the legend.
    2.Gestalt Grouping via Enclosure: If the goal is to highlight a specific period of decline (e.g., recent years), using the Gestalt principle of enclosure, such as a shaded background region—would help group those specific years together and draw the viewer's attention to the critical time period.

    **:Visualization 2: Bad Example: Suggested Improvements:** 
    1.Flatten to 2D: The most critical improvement would be to remove the 3D effect and convert the chart into a standard 2D Bar Chart. This aligns with the finding that two-dimensional images appear more objective and factual and allows for accurate comparison of the bar lengths.
    2.Simplify the Composition: Remove the background grid lines and unnecessary coloring. By simplifying the composition, we reduce the extraneous cognitive load and allow the viewer's working memory to focus on the comparison of the values rather than the chart's design elements.
      ```
